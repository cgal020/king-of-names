# Privacy and legal considerations in the UAE and Thailand for an app that stores details about third parties (status as of 7 October 2026; not legal advice)

Scope note: this covers an app where business owners record people they have met (name, phone, birthday, GPS location and time of the meeting, free-text notes, a face photo, a photo of the business card with OCR, and a photo of the place). OpenAI transcribes and extracts the data, it is stored in a cloud database, users are invited by code, and each user's data is private. Inbound WhatsApp voice notes may be added later. Each source is dated where the date is known. Anything not confirmed for 2026 is flagged. Session limits: the official UAE legislation portal returned HTTP 403, the DIFC and ADGM law texts could not be reached, and the web-search budget ran out partway through, so some points rest on secondary sources (flagged below).

## UAE federal PDPL (Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021): scope, exclusions, status of the Executive Regulations, the UAE Data Office and enforcement

### Takeaway
The PDPL is in force, but as of late September 2026 its Executive Regulations had still not been issued, according to the most recent and best-sourced checks. The UAE Data Office is not fully operational, and mainland enforcement has been minimal. The "personal purposes" exclusion is drafted much more narrowly than the GDPR household exemption: it covers a data subject processing *his/her own* data. A business owner storing other people's details for networking is therefore probably in scope, as is the app operator, which is covered even if it is based outside the UAE.

### Cited Findings
- **Exclusions in Article 2 (verbatim, as quoted by SMEX):** the PDPL "shall not apply" to "government data"; "government authorities that control or process Personal Data"; "personal Data held with security and judicial authorities"; "a data subject who processes his/her data for personal purposes"; "health personal data that is subject to legislation regulating the protection and Processing thereof"; and "banking and credit personal data and information that is subject to legislation regulating the protection and Processing thereof". — [SMEX, 25 Mar 2022](https://smex.org/uaes-data-protection-law-between-exceptions-and-exemptions/)
- SMEX notes that the law "does not clarify whether processing others' data by an individual falls outside this exclusion", which leaves it ambiguous. — [SMEX](https://smex.org/uaes-data-protection-law-between-exceptions-and-exemptions/)
- **Territorial scope:** the PDPL applies to processing of the personal data of people resident or doing business in the UAE, to controllers and processors inside the UAE, and to controllers and processors outside the UAE that process data of data subjects in the UAE. It preserves the separate regimes in the DIFC, ADGM and Dubai Healthcare City free zones, and leaves health, banking/credit and telecoms data to their own sectoral laws. — [DLA Piper, Data Protection Laws of the World: UAE](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/countries/uae-general/law.html)
- **Executive Regulations, older baseline:** DLA Piper says that "as of January 6, 2025" the regulations were unpublished, although they were due six months after the PDPL was issued on 26 September 2021. It adds that organisations get a further six months after publication to comply. — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/countries/uae-general/law.html) (FLAG: older than 2026)
- **Executive Regulations, 2026:** the Chambers 2026 UAE trends chapter states, "The Implementing Regulations, intended to clarify key aspects of the law, have yet to be issued." — [Chambers Global Practice Guides, Data Protection & Privacy 2026, UAE](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/uae/trends-and-developments)
- **Most recent check found:** "The Executive Regulations to Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 have not yet been issued: none are listed on the UAE legislation portal (checked 23 September 2026)." The same source cites Article 29: there will be a six-month window after issuance, which the Cabinet can extend. — [ITSecNow, "UAE PDPL Executive Regulations 2026 Explained"](https://itsecnow.com/regulators/pdpl-executive-regulations-2026)
- **Conflicting claims (unverified):** some secondary compliance blogs say the Executive Regulations were issued, with "Cabinet Resolution No. 33 of 2024" cited in search-engine summaries, and that there is a compliance deadline of 1 January 2027. The blog repeating the 1 January 2027 date (dated 5 Sep 2026) gives no primary source, only that the date is "widely reported". — [BinaryMinds, 5 Sep 2026](https://binaryminds.ae/blog/uae-pdpl-compliance-checklist-2027). Contradicted by [ITSecNow (portal check 23 Sep 2026)](https://itsecnow.com/regulators/pdpl-executive-regulations-2026) and by [Chambers 2026](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/uae/trends-and-developments).
- **UAE Data Office:** "the Data Office has yet to become fully operational". During its transitional period, "the TDRA has been observed to act as a point of contact". — [Chambers 2026 UAE](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/uae/trends-and-developments)
- **Enforcement reality:** Chambers describes "limited enforcement activity and a cautious regulatory stance". Organisations comply on the basis of "internal risk assessments rather than in response to regulatory action". — [Chambers 2026 UAE](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/uae/trends-and-developments)
- **Penalties:** a separate Cabinet decision is meant to set PDPL violations and administrative penalties, and has not yet been issued. Criminal exposure under the Cybercrime Law (Article 13, illegal collection/processing) is reported as fines of AED 50,000 to 500,000 or imprisonment. — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/countries/uae-general/law.html)

### Inferences
- **End user:** the Article 2 exclusion, as worded, covers a data subject processing their own data. A business owner storing third parties' details, especially for networking, probably does not fall clearly within it. Purely private use (friends, family) is ambiguous; mixed business use is very unlikely to qualify. In practice the user is best treated as a "controller" for business contacts.
- **Operator:** the operator stores, transcribes and extracts data on users' behalf. That makes it at least a processor, and a controller for anything it does for its own purposes (analytics, product improvement, feature training). The PDPL reaches the operator even if it is incorporated outside the UAE, provided it processes data of people in the UAE.
- **Enforcement reality:** without Executive Regulations, an operational regulator or a penalties decision, the risk of administrative PDPL enforcement is currently low. The criminal Cybercrime Law (next sections) is the more immediate legal exposure in the UAE, and it is actively publicised by prosecutors and judicial departments.

### Gaps
- I could not open the official PDPL text on uaelegislation.gov.ae (HTTP 403). The verbatim Article 2 wording above comes from SMEX's quotation.
- I could not trace "Cabinet Resolution No. 33 of 2024" or the "1 January 2027" deadline to any primary source (Official Gazette, the Cabinet, or the UAE Data Office). Treat both as unverified. Re-check uaelegislation.gov.ae before relying on either.
- I found no published UAE Data Office enforcement decision or fine under the PDPL up to October 2026.

## UAE PDPL substantive rules: sensitive and biometric data, consent and lawful bases, data subject rights, breach notification, cross-border transfers, DPO

### Takeaway
The PDPL is consent-first and has no "legitimate interests" basis. Sensitive data expressly includes biometric data. The biometric definition gives "facial images" as an example, which makes face photos riskier under UAE law than under Thai law. Rights broadly mirror the GDPR. Transfers abroad depend on Data Office adequacy decisions (none published) or Article 23 exceptions. A DPO is required for high-risk or large-scale sensitive processing.

### Cited Findings
- **Sensitive personal data** covers information that "directly or indirectly" reveals family background, race, political or philosophical views, religious beliefs, criminal history, biometric data, or health, including "physical, psychological, mental, genetic or sexual condition". — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/countries/uae-general/law.html)
- **Biometric data:** personal data resulting from processing, using a specific technique, relating to the physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics of a data subject that allows or confirms their unique identification, "such as facial images or dactyloscopic data". — [search-result summary of SMEX / Bird & Bird material](https://smex.org/uaes-data-protection-law-between-exceptions-and-exemptions/); also [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/countries/uae-general/law.html) ("facial images, fingerprint data")
- **Lawful bases:** processing requires consent unless it is necessary for one of the listed purposes: employment and social security obligations, contract performance, protecting the data subject's interests, public interest, legal proceedings, medical purposes, public health, archival/scientific/statistical purposes, or other statutory obligations. — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/countries/uae-general/law.html)
- "The PDPL fails to include legitimate interests as a legal basis for processing personal data." — [SMEX](https://smex.org/uaes-data-protection-law-between-exceptions-and-exemptions/)
- **Data subject rights:** access (types of data and purposes), portability, correction or erasure, restriction, stopping processing, and objection to automated decisions and profiling. DLA Piper reports a one-month response period, extendable by two months for complex requests. — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/countries/uae-general/law.html)
- **Breach notification:** controllers must report breaches to the Data Office "immediately upon becoming aware", following procedures to be set in the (unissued) Executive Regulations. — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/countries/uae-general/law.html)
- **Cross-border transfers:** Articles 22 and 23 allow transfers to jurisdictions that the Data Office approves as having "sufficient provisions, measures, controls, requirements and rules for protecting privacy". Article 23 contains exceptions. — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/countries/uae-general/law.html). A secondary, AI-assisted consultancy document (March 2026) summarises the alternatives as "appropriate contractual safeguards" or "explicit consent". — [Reina Consulting via Primerus, 23 Mar 2026](https://www.primerus.com/sites/default/files/2026-03/UAE%20Data%20Privacy%20Law.pdf) (FLAG: low-reliability source)
- **DPO:** mandatory where processing involves a high-risk, systematic assessment of sensitive data or profiling, or large volumes of sensitive personal data. The DPO can be staff or a contractor and need not be based in the UAE. — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/countries/uae-general/law.html)

### Inferences
- **No legitimate interests basis:** for UAE data subjects, the operator and the user have to rely on consent or on a listed exception, such as steps toward a contract. "Business networking" does not map cleanly onto any of them. A business card that is handed over voluntarily is the strongest available argument for implied, purpose-limited consent to be contacted. A face photo taken without consent is the weakest point.
- **Biometric definition:** it combines "using a specific technique" with the example "facial images". The best reading, matching the GDPR's equivalent definition, is that an ordinary photo becomes biometric only once it is processed technically to identify someone. The PDPL has no clarifying recital, though, so a cautious operator should treat stored face photos as close to sensitive data.
- **Cross-border transfers:** no UAE adequacy list has been published, and the Executive Regulations are not out. Sending UAE residents' data to OpenAI (US) and to a foreign cloud therefore currently rests on Article 23 exceptions and contracts. Collecting explicit consent from the third parties themselves is not practical.

### Gaps
- I could not verify the verbatim Article 23 exceptions, the Article 9 notification to data subjects, or the exact time limits for responding to rights requests from the official text.
- I found no UAE guidance specifically on photos as biometric data.

## UAE free zones: DIFC Data Protection Law No. 5 of 2020 (with the 2025 amendments) and ADGM Data Protection Regulations 2021

### Takeaway
The DIFC and ADGM have their own GDPR-style regimes with active regulators. In 2025 the DIFC widened its reach and created a private right of action. These regimes matter only if the operator (or a user acting for a business) is established or operating in the DIFC or ADGM. Otherwise the federal PDPL applies.

### Cited Findings
- The **DIFC Amendment Law No. 1 of 2025** took effect on **15 July 2025**. Article 6 now applies the law to (a) DIFC-incorporated entities processing data anywhere, and (b) any controller or processor carrying on activities in the DIFC as part of stable arrangements. A new Article 64A gives data subjects a direct right to sue in the DIFC Courts for financial and non-financial damage such as distress. — [Baker McKenzie, Connect on Tech](https://connectontech.bakermckenzie.com/dubai-international-financial-centre-updates-data-protection-law/)
- **DIFC fines changed in 2025:** a new maximum of USD 25,000 for failing mandatory annual assessments; DPIA violations raised from USD 20,000 to USD 50,000; data-sharing disclosure violations raised from USD 10,000 to USD 50,000. — [Baker McKenzie](https://connectontech.bakermckenzie.com/dubai-international-financial-centre-updates-data-protection-law/)
- Bird & Bird's summary (via search) says the amendments extend the law to all processing within the DIFC regardless of where the entity is incorporated. They also require a documented assessment, before transferring data out of the DIFC, of whether data subjects will have adequate protection and effective remedies in the recipient jurisdiction. — [Bird & Bird, 2025](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/united-arab-emirates/difc-enacts-amendments-to-data-protection-law)
- Chambers describes the DIFC change as extending scope to controllers processing data of DIFC residents regardless of incorporation. This conflicts slightly with Baker McKenzie's "stable arrangements" wording. — [Chambers 2026 UAE](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/uae/trends-and-developments); compare [Baker McKenzie](https://connectontech.bakermckenzie.com/dubai-international-financial-centre-updates-data-protection-law/)
- **DIFC Regulation 10** (September 2023) covers autonomous and AI systems and requires transparency and accountability. — [Chambers 2026 UAE](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/uae/trends-and-developments)
- In **ADGM**, the Data Protection Regulations (Substantial Public Interest Conditions) Rules 2025 (September 2025) cover processing of special categories for insurance and for safeguarding vulnerable individuals. — [Chambers 2026 UAE](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/uae/trends-and-developments)
- The ADGM Office of Data Protection says "all ADGM registered entities that process personal data are required to register as a Data Controller". — [ADGM Office of Data Protection](https://www.adgm.com/operating-in-adgm/office-of-data-protection)
- Chambers notes that, under existing free-zone frameworks, biometric data falls within "special categories" requiring explicit consent. — [Chambers 2026 UAE](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/uae/trends-and-developments)

### Inferences
- If the operator incorporates in the DIFC or ADGM, it takes on a stricter, actively enforced GDPR-style regime. That includes, since July 2025, DIFC private lawsuits for distress. It also gets clearer concepts: legitimate interests, household exemption, and biometric data defined by purpose. For an app built around non-consenting third parties, a mainland entity under the as-yet unenforced PDPL carries less near-term regulatory risk. A free-zone entity offers more legal certainty but higher enforcement risk.
- An end user who is a DIFC or ADGM firm, recording contacts for that firm, is likely a controller under the free-zone law.

### Gaps
- I could not open the DIFC DPL 2020 or ADGM DPR 2021 texts (404s), so these are **not verified in this session**: the verbatim household exemptions (believed to exclude processing by natural persons "in the course of a purely personal or household activity"), the special-category definitions (believed to cover biometric data "for the purpose of uniquely identifying" a person), and the legitimate-interest wording. Check them against the DIFC and ADGM legislation pages.
- I found no DIFC or ADGM enforcement decision specifically about contact-management or face-photo apps.

## UAE criminal law on photographing people and storing their images and information without consent (Cybercrime Law No. 34 of 2021, Penal Code)

### Takeaway
Article 44 of the Cybercrime Law (Federal Decree-Law No. 34 of 2021) makes it an offence to use an information network, electronic information system or IT means to infringe someone's privacy without consent. Listed conduct includes taking photos of others in public or private places and retaining electronic images. The penalty is at least six months' imprisonment and/or a fine of AED 150,000 to 500,000. This is the biggest UAE legal risk in the "photograph the person you just met" feature.

### Cited Findings
- Under Article 44, using an information network, electronic information system or IT means "with the intent to infringe upon the privacy of a person or the sanctity of private or family life without their consent and in circumstances not legally permitted" is punishable by "imprisonment for a period of no less than six (6) months and a fine of no less than 150,000 AED and no more than 500,000 AED", or either penalty. — [BSA Law (Saad Younes), 25 Sep 2024](https://bsalaw.com/insight/legal-risks-of-photography-and-technology-use-navigating-privacy-laws-under-federal-decree-law-no-34-of-2021/)
- The listed acts include "Taking photographs of others in any public or private place, or preparing, transmitting, revealing, copying, or retaining electronic images"; publishing news, photos or data "with the intent to harm the person"; capturing and sharing images of injured or dead people; and tracking or disclosing geographical location data. — [BSA Law](https://bsalaw.com/insight/legal-risks-of-photography-and-technology-use-navigating-privacy-laws-under-federal-decree-law-no-34-of-2021/)
- Modifying recordings or images in order to defame carries at least one year's imprisonment and a fine of AED 250,000 to 500,000. — [BSA Law](https://bsalaw.com/insight/legal-risks-of-photography-and-technology-use-navigating-privacy-laws-under-federal-decree-law-no-34-of-2021/); [Lexis Middle East, 21 Dec 2023](https://www.lexis.ae/2023/12/21/auh-penalties-for-privacy-violations-clarified/)
- On 17 December 2023 the Abu Dhabi Judicial Department publicly restated these penalties, including for "Taking or revealing images of others without permission" and "Tracking or monitoring others' locations without authorization". — [Lexis Middle East](https://www.lexis.ae/2023/12/21/auh-penalties-for-privacy-violations-clarified/)
- BSA's commentary stresses an intent element. It says that "if the person believes they are fulfilling a legal duty, such as reporting a crime or documenting an illegal act, then malicious intent is negated." — [BSA Law](https://bsalaw.com/insight/legal-risks-of-photography-and-technology-use-navigating-privacy-laws-under-federal-decree-law-no-34-of-2021/)

### Inferences
- **"Take a photo of the person you just met":** the statute expressly covers taking photos of others *in public places* and *retaining* electronic images without consent. A covert or unconsented snapshot stored in a cloud app is the kind of conduct the article describes. There is a defence argument based on the intent element ("intent to infringe privacy"), but it is uncertain and fact-specific. The practical rule for UAE users is to **photograph a person only with their clear, preferably recorded, consent**. Otherwise, use an existing consensual image, such as a LinkedIn photo they published or one they sent, or no photo at all.
- **Other photo types:**
  - Business-card photo: low risk. It is an image of a document the person handed over, not of the person.
  - Place photo: low risk, provided identifiable bystanders are not the subject.
- **GPS:** the app records the *user's own* location at the meeting. That is not "tracking" the other person, but sharing or exporting it alongside the contact could be argued to disclose that person's whereabouts. Keep it private to the user.
- **Operator exposure:** the operator could face accessory risk if the app markets or encourages covert photography. Product design and terms of use should therefore require consent for face photos.

### Gaps
- I could not verify the Penal Code (Federal Decree-Law No. 31 of 2021) privacy articles, such as the provision on photographing people in private places, because the search budget ran out. Confirm the article number and text before relying on it.
- I found no reported prosecution involving a networking or contact-notes app. Published cases and warnings mostly concern posting images on social media.

## Thailand PDPA B.E. 2562 (2019): scope and household exemption, lawful bases, sensitive and biometric data, notice when data comes from other sources, cross-border transfers, DPO

### Takeaway
The PDPA is GDPR-like and, unlike the UAE PDPL, includes **legitimate interests** (Section 24(5)). The household exemption covers collection "for personal benefit or household activity of such Person only", so mixed business and personal use likely falls outside it. Biometric data is defined by technology (e.g. "facial recognition data"), so a plain face photo is not biometric until it is used for face recognition. Data collected from someone other than the data subject triggers notice duties under Section 25. The PDPA also applies to foreign operators that offer services to people in Thailand.

### Cited Findings
- **Section 4(1):** the Act does not apply to "the collection, use, or disclosure of Personal Data by a Person who collects such Personal Data for personal benefit or household activity of such Person only". — [PDPA, unofficial English translation (Government Gazette 27 May 2019)](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)
- **Section 5 (extraterritorial reach):** the Act applies to controllers and processors outside Thailand who process data of data subjects in Thailand when "(1) the offering of goods or services to the data subjects who are in the Kingdom of Thailand, irrespective of whether the payment is made" or "(2) the monitoring of the data subject's behavior" in Thailand. — [PDPA translation](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)
- **Section 22, data minimisation:** collection "shall be limited to the extent necessary in relation to the lawful purpose". — [PDPA translation](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)
- **Section 23, notice:** notice is required "prior to or at the time of such collection", covering purpose, the data collected and the retention period, recipients, contact details, and rights, "except the case where the data subject already knows of such details". — [PDPA translation](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)
- **Section 24, lawful bases other than consent:** these include contract (24(3)) and "(5) it is necessary for legitimate interests of the Data Controller or any other Persons or juristic persons other than the Data Controller, except where such interests are overridden by the fundamental rights of the data subject". — [PDPA translation](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)
- **Section 25, collection from other sources:** this is prohibited unless (1) the controller has informed the data subject "without delay, but shall not exceed thirty days" and obtained consent, or (2) the collection falls within the Section 24 or 26 exceptions. Notice details must be given "within thirty days after the date of collection", except that "the Personal Data are to be used for communication with the data subject, the notice of information details shall be provided at the time of the first communication". — [PDPA translation](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)
- **Section 26, sensitive data:** "racial, ethnic origin, political opinions, cult, religious or philosophical beliefs, sexual behavior, criminal records, health data, disability, trade union information, genetic data, biometric data" cannot be collected "without the explicit consent", subject to narrow exceptions (vital interests, legal claims, specific public-interest and health purposes). Legitimate interests is not one of them. — [PDPA translation](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)
- **Biometric data definition (Section 26):** "the Personal Data arising from the use of technics or technology related to the physical or behavioral dominance of Person, which can be used to identify such Person apart from other Persons, such as the facial recognition data, iris recognition data or fingerprint recognition data." — [PDPA translation](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)
- **Section 28, cross-border transfers:** the destination must have an "adequate data protection standard" under PDPC rules. Exceptions include consent after the data subject is informed of inadequate standards, contract necessity, and vital interests. Section 29 allows intra-group certified policies (BCRs) or "suitable protection measures which enable the enforcement of the data subject's rights". — [PDPA translation](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)
- **Section 32, right to object:** data subjects can object to processing based on Section 24(4) or (5) unless the controller shows "a compelling legitimate ground" or a legal-claims need, and they can always object to direct marketing. **Section 33, erasure:** data subjects can request erasure, destruction or anonymisation, including when consent is withdrawn or data was unlawfully collected. — [PDPA translation](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)
- **Section 37, security and breach:** appropriate security measures are required, and the controller must "notify the Office of any Personal Data breach without delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours". High-risk breaches must also be notified to data subjects. Under **Section 37(5)**, a foreign controller caught by Section 5 must appoint a representative in Thailand. Under Section 38, that does not apply to controllers that do not process Section 26 data and do not hold "a large amount of Personal Data". — [PDPA translation](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)
- **Section 40:** processors need an agreement with the controller (the DPA), and certain record-keeping can be waived for small organisations unless processing is risky, regular or involves Section 26 data. — [PDPA translation](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)
- **Section 41, DPO:** a DPO is required where (2) processing "require[s] a regular monitoring of the Personal Data or the system, by the reason of having a large number of Personal Data", or where (3) "the core activity ... is the collection, use, or disclosure of the Personal Data according to section 26". — [PDPA translation](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)
- **PDPC DPO Notification** (published 14 Sep 2023, effective 13 Dec 2023): "100,000 data subjects or more" counts as large-scale. "Core activity" means an "essential and integral activity directly related to the primary operations". Regular monitoring covers "tracking, monitoring, analyzing, or predicting the behavior, attitude, or profile of individuals". — [Tilleke & Gibbins](https://www.tilleke.com/insights/thailand-releases-notification-on-data-protection-officer-appointment/16/)
- **PDPC cross-border Notifications** under Sections 28 and 29 (issued 25 Dec 2023, effective 24 Mar 2024): "there is currently no specific country listed by the regulator as a whitelisted country". Valid safeguards include SCCs, either with the minimum clauses or following the "ASEAN Model Contractual Clauses ... (ii) EU Standard Contractual Clauses", as well as BCRs and certification. — [Baker McKenzie via Global Compliance News, 29 Feb 2024](https://www.globalcompliancenews.com/2024/02/29/https-insightplus-bakermckenzie-com-bm-investigations-compliance-ethics-thailand-new-cross-border-data-transfer-rules-officially-published-as-law_02092024/); [Tilleke & Gibbins](https://www.tilleke.com/insights/thailands-regulations-for-cross-border-personal-data-transfer-come-into-force/)
- **Administrative fines:** up to THB 1 million (e.g., notice failures under Sections 23 and 41), up to THB 3 million (e.g., Sections 24, 25, 28 and 37), and up to THB 5 million for Section 26 (sensitive data) violations and sensitive-data transfers. — [PDPA translation, Sections 82-84](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)
- **Civil and criminal liability:** controllers are liable for damage "regardless of whether such operation is performed intentionally or negligently" (Section 77), and courts can award punitive damages of up to twice actual compensation (Section 78). Misuse of sensitive data likely to cause damage or humiliation carries up to six months' imprisonment and/or THB 500,000; for unlawful gain, up to one year and/or THB 1 million (Section 79). — [PDPA translation](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)

### Inferences
- **End user:** a business owner using the app partly for networking is not collecting "for personal benefit or household activity ... only". For business contacts they are most likely a **data controller**. The exemption plausibly covers purely private entries (friends, family), so the app could let users tag contacts "personal" or "business".
- **Operator:** it is a processor for users' entries and a controller for its own uses. If it is outside Thailand but offers the app to people in Thailand, Section 5 applies. A Thai representative under Section 37(5) is needed unless the Section 38 carve-out applies (no sensitive data, not a large amount). Adding face recognition would remove that carve-out.
- **Section 25:** when a user records a third party based on a conversation or another person's introduction, the data is collected "from other sources" from the operator's point of view. The user-as-controller can rely on legitimate interest (Section 24(5)), so consent is not needed, but the Section 23 notice must then be given within 30 days or at the "first communication". In practice, users who follow up with a contact should identify themselves and how they got the details. The app can help, for example with a short privacy line in follow-up message templates.
- **Legitimate interests** makes Thailand much friendlier than the UAE for a contact-notes app, as long as the data stays proportionate. Notes that reveal health, religion, political views, sexual behaviour or criminal records are Section 26 data and need explicit consent. Users should be warned not to record them.

### Gaps
- The text used is an unofficial English translation. Only the Thai text is legally binding.
- I did not find Thai PDPC guidance specifically on whether ordinary photos of people are personal data needing a lawful basis when taken by individuals. It is commonly reported that PDPC officials said in 2022 that casual personal photography is outside the PDPA, but I could not verify this in this session.

## Thailand: PDPC subordinate rules for small businesses, enforcement and fines 2024-2026, and 2025-2026 developments

### Takeaway
SMEs are exempt from most record-keeping, but not if they process sensitive data or process regularly and riskily. Enforcement became real in 2024-2025, with multi-million-baht fines focused on security failures, breach notification and missing DPOs. The PDPC also acted against biometric (iris) processing in November 2025. New guidance (2026 consultation), certification of binding corporate rules (Sept 2025), and new criminal data-misuse offences (April 2025) mean regulation is tightening.

### Cited Findings
- **SME ROPA exemption:** the PDPC Notification on Exemption of the Record of Processing Activities Requirement for Data Controllers who are Small Businesses B.E. 2565 (2022), effective 21 June 2022, exempts SMEs, community enterprises and social enterprises from Section 39 records. Records of rejected data-subject requests must still be kept. The exemption does not apply where processing may put rights at risk, is regular rather than occasional, or involves Section 26 sensitive data. — [Forvis Mazars Thailand (via search summary)](https://forvismazars.com/th/en/insights/doing-business-in-thailand/legal/small-enterprises-exempt-from-preparing-records)
- **First fine (2024):** "the first issuance of fines occurred last year, when the PDPC penalized data controllers for their failure to provide appropriate security measures, notify the PDPC of the data breach, and appoint a Data Protection Officer...with fines totaling THB 7,000,000." — [Nagashima Ohno & Tsunematsu, 22 Aug 2025](https://www.nagashima.com/en/publications/publication20250822-1/)
- **1 August 2025 announcement:** eight fines across five cases were announced:
  - a government agency and its developer, THB 153,120 each;
  - a private hospital, THB 1,210,000, and an individual contractor, THB 16,940;
  - a computer retailer, THB 7,000,000 (no DPO, no breach notification, inadequate security);
  - a cosmetics company, THB 2,500,000;
  - a toy retailer, THB 500,000, and its processor, THB 3,000,000.
  — [Nagashima](https://www.nagashima.com/en/publications/publication20250822-1/); [Chambers 2026 Thailand (Chandler Mori Hamada, updated 10 Mar 2026)](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/thailand/trends-and-developments)
- **Conflict on totals:** some firms describe the August 2025 round as "approximately THB 21.5 million". — [Gala Law blog (search summary)](https://blog.galalaw.com/post/102lvee/thailands-pdpc-signals-tougher-enforcement-with-multi-million-baht-fines); [Chambers 2026 Thailand](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/thailand/trends-and-developments). Nagashima puts the eight August 2025 fines at about THB 14.5 million and gives THB 21.5 million as the *cumulative* total including the 2024 fine. — [Nagashima](https://www.nagashima.com/en/publications/publication20250822-1/). My arithmetic on the eight listed amounts gives about THB 14.53 million, which supports Nagashima. Both the 2024 fine and the 2025 computer-retailer fine are THB 7 million for the same three failures, so it is possible they are the same case counted twice.
- **Common thread:** breach-reporting failures made up THB 9.5 million of the fines across two of the five cases. — [Enersys (search summary)](https://enersys.co.th/en/insights/thailand-pdpa-enforcement-2026-five-cases-lessons)
- **Biometrics action, 24 November 2025:** the PDPC ordered a halt to iris scanning and deletion of biometric data for about 1.2 million users in the World/Worldcoin case. It cited concerns about whether consent was freely given and about inadequate safeguards. — [Chambers 2026 Thailand](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/thailand/trends-and-developments)
- **BCR certification, 29 September 2025:** the PDPC issued rules for certifying intra-group data protection policies (BCR-C and BCR-P), and the first two approvals followed on 30 September 2025. — [Chambers 2026 Thailand](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/thailand/trends-and-developments)
- **Emergency Decree on Measures for the Prevention and Suppression of Technology Crimes (No. 2) B.E. 2568 (2025)**, effective 13 April 2025, created personal-data-misuse offences: up to one year's imprisonment or THB 100,000, rising to up to five years or THB 500,000 for commercial exploitation. — [Chambers 2026 Thailand](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/thailand/trends-and-developments)
- **PDPA amendment consultation (2025)** covered controller and processor definitions, sensitive data categories, consent voluntariness, and the regulator's framework. A PDPC "data protection standard mark" (trust mark) was being prepared for announcement in Q2-Q3 2026. — [Chambers 2026 Thailand](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/thailand/trends-and-developments)
- **March 2026 consultation:** "In March 2026, the PDPC opened a public consultation to shape a forthcoming set of PDPA guidelines". It covers legal bases, security and breach notification, DPO obligations, marketing and direct marketing, ROPA, and CCTV and ID-card collection. Drafts are expected about eight months after the consultation closed on 23 March 2026. — [Cookie Information blog (vendor), 2026](https://cookieinformation.com/blog/what-is-the-thailand-pdpa/)

### Inferences
- **SME exemption:** a small operator, or SME end users, could rely on the ROPA exemption only while no sensitive data (e.g., face recognition) is processed. Because the app processes regularly, the exemption is already doubtful.
- **Enforcement priorities:** fines so far target security, breach notification and missing DPOs, not the "lawful basis" questions. The most important controls for an operator storing third-party data are encryption, access control, a written processor agreement with every vendor, and a 72-hour breach process.
- **Biometrics:** the Worldcoin action shows the PDPC will act on biometric processing it sees as lacking freely given consent. That is directly relevant if face matching is added later.

### Gaps
- A search snippet mentioned a PDPC notification on Section 30 access rights published 16 July 2026, but I could not trace it to a source. It is unverified.
- I found no PDPC fines published after 2025 and up to October 2026. That may be a gap in my search (the budget ran out) rather than a sign that none were issued.
- The status of the 2025 draft PDPA amendments (passed or pending) as of October 2026 is unverified.

## Thailand: Computer Crime Act and other laws on photographing people and storing their images

### Takeaway
Thailand has no verified equivalent of the UAE's broad Article 44 ban on photographing people. The main criminal risks are creating or editing images of a person in a defamatory way (Computer Crime Act) and, since April 2025, misusing personal data under the Technology Crimes Emergency Decree. The PDPA itself carries criminal penalties for misusing sensitive data.

### Cited Findings
- The 2025 Technology Crimes Emergency Decree (No. 2) adds criminal offences for personal-data misuse: up to one year or THB 100,000, and up to five years or THB 500,000 for commercial exploitation. — [Chambers 2026 Thailand](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/thailand/trends-and-developments)
- PDPA Section 79 makes it a crime to misuse or transfer sensitive data (which includes biometric data) in a way likely to damage, defame or humiliate someone, or for unlawful gain. — [PDPA translation](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)

### Inferences
- In Thailand, taking a face photo with the person's knowledge, keeping it privately and not editing or publishing it carries much lower legal risk than in the UAE. It remains personal data subject to the PDPA if the user is a controller (business use). The practical guidance is still to ask permission.

### Gaps
- I could not verify the text of Section 16 of the Computer-related Crime Act B.E. 2550 (as amended B.E. 2560), on images of others that are created, edited or altered to defame, or its penalties (the Wikipedia page returned 404 and the search budget was exhausted).
- I could not verify Thai Criminal Code defamation and privacy provisions relevant to photos, or any Thai court cases on photographing people in public.

## Both jurisdictions: how business card information is treated, and whether OCR changes anything

### Takeaway
No UAE or Thai regulator guidance specifically on business cards was found. Under the statutory frameworks, a card handed over voluntarily for business contact is the lowest-risk data in the app. In Thailand, it supports legitimate interest (and arguably implied awareness under Section 23). In the UAE, it is the best available argument for implied consent, limited to business contact. OCR does not change the legal category. What matters is the AI-provider transfer and any later re-purposing.

### Cited Findings
- **Thailand:** Section 23's notice duty does not apply where "the data subject already knows of such details". Legitimate interest is a lawful basis (Section 24(5)), and data subjects may object to legitimate-interest processing and always to direct marketing (Section 32). — [PDPA translation](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)
- **UAE:** there is no legitimate-interests basis, so processing relies on consent or listed exceptions. — [SMEX](https://smex.org/uaes-data-protection-law-between-exceptions-and-exemptions/); [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/countries/uae-general/law.html)

### Inferences
- A business card is collected directly from the data subject, which is the opposite of a Section 25 "other source" collection. The card holder's purpose in handing it over (being contacted about business) sets the outer limit of compatible use. Adding the contact to marketing lists, or sharing it with others, goes beyond that purpose. In Thailand it would trigger the Section 32 objection right for direct marketing.
- **OCR:** extracting text by OCR only digitises what the person handed over, so it creates no new data category. Two things do change:
  - The card image and its text go to OpenAI, a processor that may be abroad. That is a cross-border transfer needing Section 28/29 safeguards in Thailand and Article 22/23 grounds in the UAE.
  - Enrichment, such as having the model infer seniority or company details or look the person up online, would be new processing that should be disclosed and minimised.
- Birthdays, personal notes and face photos go beyond what a business card implies. They need their own justification: legitimate interest and proportionality in Thailand, and consent or an exception in the UAE.

### Gaps
- I found no UAE or Thai regulator guidance on business cards, contact-management apps or OCR specifically. The points above are statutory analysis, not regulator positions.

## Both jurisdictions: is a plain face photo "biometric data", and what changes if face recognition or face search is added?

### Takeaway
In Thailand, a plain photo is **not** biometric data. The statutory definition requires data "arising from the use of technics or technology" that can identify someone, "such as the facial recognition data". In the UAE, the definition also requires processing "using a specific technique", but it lists "facial images" as an example, so a cautious reading treats stored face photos as close to sensitive. In both countries, adding face recognition or face search turns the photos into biometric sensitive data. In Thailand that requires **explicit consent from each person photographed**, which the app cannot obtain from non-users. That makes face search on non-consenting third parties effectively unlawful in Thailand and very high-risk in the UAE.

### Cited Findings
- **Thailand:** biometric data means data "arising from the use of technics or technology ... which can be used to identify such Person ... such as the facial recognition data". Its collection is prohibited "without the explicit consent", and legitimate interest is not an available exception. — [PDPA translation, Section 26](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)
- **Thailand:** fines for Section 26 violations go up to THB 5 million (Section 84), with criminal liability under Section 79. A DPO is required if Section 26 processing is a "core activity" (Section 41(3)). — [PDPA translation](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)
- **Thailand:** in November 2025 the PDPC ordered deletion of biometric (iris) data of about 1.2 million users over concerns about consent voluntariness. — [Chambers 2026 Thailand](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/thailand/trends-and-developments)
- **UAE:** biometric data, with examples including "facial images", is listed as sensitive personal data. A DPO is required for high-risk systematic assessment of sensitive data or large volumes of it. — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/countries/uae-general/law.html)
- **UAE free zones:** biometric data is a "special category" requiring explicit consent. — [Chambers 2026 UAE](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/uae/trends-and-developments)

### Inferences
- **Today (photo as a memory aid only):**
  - Thailand: ordinary personal data under the PDPA. Legitimate interest is arguable if the photo was taken openly with the person's knowledge.
  - UAE: the main problem is Cybercrime Law Article 44 if the photo was taken without consent, more than PDPL classification.
- **Later (face recognition, face search, "who is this?" matching, deduplicating contacts by face):**
  - Thailand: explicit consent is needed from every person in the photo. This is practically impossible for third parties, so the feature should not be built for non-consenting people. It also takes the operator out of the Section 38 representative carve-out and probably triggers a mandatory DPO.
  - UAE: sensitive data, likely DPO and DPIA-type obligations once the Executive Regulations arrive, plus Article 44 exposure.
  - The UAE federal transfer rules and the DIFC 2025 transfer-assessment duty would also apply to biometric templates sent abroad.
- **Sending face photos to a vision model:** asking a general multimodal model (e.g., OpenAI) to *describe* or *transcribe* a photo is not, in itself, biometric identification. Asking it to *identify* or *match* a face would be. Prompts and vendor settings should ensure no identification occurs. This is an inference; vendor terms should be checked.
- **Voice notes (future WhatsApp feature):** transcription alone is not biometric. Using voiceprints to identify speakers would be.

### Gaps
- I found no UAE or Thai regulator guidance explicitly saying that ordinary photographs are or are not biometric data. The GDPR-style reading (photos are biometric only when specifically processed) is an inference here, not a verified UAE position.

## Practical recommendations for the app operator (privacy notice, purpose limitation, deletion on request, data minimisation, encryption, retention, vendor DPA with the AI provider, data residency)

### Takeaway
Treat each user as a controller for their contacts and the operator as their processor, with the operator as controller for its own account and analytics data. Build consent prompts for face photos, minimisation, deletion tooling and a strong vendor and transfer setup. Leave out face recognition. Thailand's enforcement record says to prioritise security, breach response and DPAs. The UAE's Cybercrime Law says to prioritise consent for photos.

### Cited Findings
- **OpenAI API data controls:** API data is "not used to train or improve OpenAI models (unless you explicitly opt in)". Abuse-monitoring logs are kept "for up to 30 days". Eligible customers can get Modified Abuse Monitoring or Zero Data Retention. — [OpenAI API docs, "Your data"](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/your-data)
- **OpenAI data residency** (storage) regions: United States, Europe (EEA and Switzerland), Australia, Canada, Japan, India, Singapore, South Korea, United Kingdom and **United Arab Emirates**. Only the US and Europe support in-region *processing*. In other regions, "OpenAI may also process and temporarily store Customer Content outside of the Region". Non-US regions require approval for abuse-monitoring controls and a Modified Retention amendment. Eligible endpoints include `/v1/audio/transcriptions`, `/v1/responses` and `/v1/chat/completions`. — [OpenAI API docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/your-data)
- **Thailand:** processors must act under a controller agreement (Section 40), and controllers must stop recipients misusing data (Section 37(2)). — [PDPA translation](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf)
- **Thailand transfers:** no whitelist exists, and SCCs (ASEAN MCCs or EU SCCs) are accepted safeguards. — [Baker McKenzie via GCN](https://www.globalcompliancenews.com/2024/02/29/https-insightplus-bakermckenzie-com-bm-investigations-compliance-ethics-thailand-new-cross-border-data-transfer-rules-officially-published-as-law_02092024/)
- **Thailand fines:** both the 2024-2025 fines on processors (THB 3 million on a toy retailer's processor) and the fine on a controller for having "no Data Processing Agreement" (the government agency case) show the PDPC holds processors and missing DPAs liable. — [Nagashima](https://www.nagashima.com/en/publications/publication20250822-1/); [Chambers 2026 Thailand](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/thailand/trends-and-developments)
- **Breach timelines:** Thailand requires notice within 72 hours where feasible (Section 37(4)) — [PDPA translation](https://pdpa.msu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/EN-PDPA-2019.pdf). The UAE requires notice "immediately upon becoming aware" — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/countries/uae-general/law.html).

### Inferences (practical checklist; not legal advice)
1. **Roles and terms.** Terms of use should make users responsible for having a lawful basis and for complying with local law. They should explicitly require consent before photographing a person, given UAE Cybercrime Law Article 44. A processor agreement (DPA) between the operator and each user should cover security, sub-processors (OpenAI, cloud, WhatsApp), deletion and breach assistance.
2. **Privacy notice, in two layers.**
   - For users: what the app collects, the AI processing, sub-processors and the countries they are in, retention, and rights.
   - A short public "If you are someone recorded in this app" page: what may be held, why, how to object or request deletion, and a contact point. This serves UAE and Thai data-subject rights and the Thai Section 23/25 notice duties.
3. **Purpose limitation.** Use stored contacts only to help the user remember and follow up. No marketing, enrichment, cross-user sharing, people search, or model training on contact content. Keep OpenAI's no-training default, and consider Zero Data Retention or Modified Abuse Monitoring.
4. **Minimisation by field.**
   - Face photo: make it optional, behind an "I have this person's permission" checkbox. Ideally default to off in the UAE.
   - Place photo: discourage identifiable bystanders.
   - GPS: store a coarse location by default (venue or neighbourhood), not continuous tracks.
   - Notes: warn in the UI against recording health, religion, politics, sexuality or criminal matters (Thai Section 26 and UAE sensitive data). Consider a model-side filter that flags them.
5. **No face recognition, face search or voiceprint identification** of third parties. If ever considered, limit it to consenting people (e.g., contacts who are themselves users and opt in) and do a DPIA first.
6. **Deletion and rights tooling.** One-tap deletion of a contact with cascade to images, transcripts, embeddings, OCR output and backups (with a backup expiry window). A user-level "delete my account and all data". An operator workflow to find and delete records about a requesting third party across users while keeping users' data private. This last one is hard and needs careful design: for example, the operator forwards the request to affected users or removes matching records by phone number or email. Respond within one month (UAE, per DLA Piper) and keep records of refusals (Thailand, Sections 30 and 32).
7. **Retention.** Set a default, e.g., prompt the user to review contacts with no activity after 24 months. Purge raw audio and voice notes once transcribed. Keep OpenAI-side retention minimal.
8. **Security.** Encryption in transit and at rest. Per-user tenant isolation (row-level security), checked with tests. Encrypted object storage for images, with short-lived signed URLs. Invite codes that expire. MFA option. Audit logs. A 72-hour breach runbook, given Thailand's fines for late notification.
9. **Cross-border transfers and data residency.**
   - OpenAI offers UAE *storage* residency but not UAE *processing*. Thailand is not a residency region. Data from both countries will therefore be processed abroad (US or EU).
   - For Thai data: execute OpenAI's DPA and SCCs (ASEAN MCCs or EU SCCs are accepted forms). Do the same with the cloud database provider.
   - For UAE data: document reliance on Article 23 exceptions and contracts while no adequacy list exists. For DIFC-scoped processing, write up the documented transfer assessment required since July 2025.
   - Consider hosting the database in-region (e.g., a UAE or Singapore cloud region) to reduce the transfer footprint.
10. **Thailand representative and DPO.** If the operator is outside Thailand but targets Thai users, consider whether a Thai representative is needed (Section 37(5)). It is avoidable under Section 38 only while there is no sensitive data and volume is low. A DPO is not required at small scale without sensitive core processing; reassess at 100,000 data subjects or if biometrics are added.
11. **WhatsApp voice notes (future).** Inbound voice notes add Meta as a channel. Voice notes may capture third parties' voices and data, so treat them like notes: transcribe, minimise, then delete the audio.
12. **Watch list for the next six to twelve months:**
    - UAE PDPL Executive Regulations and the penalties Cabinet decision (six-month compliance window once issued);
    - PDPC guidelines from the March 2026 consultation (legal bases, ROPA, DPO);
    - the Thai PDPA amendment bill;
    - the Thai trust mark.

### Gaps
- I did not review OpenAI's current DPA text or sub-processor list, or WhatsApp Business terms, for 2026. Check them directly.
- I found no regulator guidance in either country on how an operator should handle third-party deletion requests across private user accounts.
